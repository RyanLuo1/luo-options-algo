Why the number is real

Every option has two prices at once: what buyers will pay and what sellers will take. Most tools quote the last trade, which can be hours old. We quote the side you’d actually transact on.

When you sell, we use the bid. When you buy, we use the ask. So the credit is what a broker would actually fill.

Three moves. One trade.

The screener looks for one structure only: three legs you can set for income or for upside, with a defined best case and a downside you choose in advance.

1

Buy a call near the stock price

This is the part of the trade that gains if the stock rises. You pay for it, at the ask.

2

Sell a higher call

This caps how much you can make, and in return someone pays you a premium, at the bid.

3

Sell a put below

You collect another premium. If the stock falls to that price, you agree to own it there.

What this is, and isn’t

It is

  • Priced at the real bid and ask. The credit on screen is one a broker could fill.
  • A scan of about 120 large-cap stocks, twice a day. Plus any list you type in yourself.
  • Plain dollars. Probability, the collateral a trade ties up, and the downside, all spelled out.

It isn’t

  • Advice. It finds and prices a structure. Whether to trade it is yours to decide.
  • A promise. A 53% probability is an estimate from today’s prices, not a return.
  • Risk-free. The put means you may end up owning the stock, at a price you picked in advance.
