Why the number is real.

Most screeners quote the last trade, which can be hours old and quietly too generous. We don't. When you sell, we use the bid. When you buy, we use the ask. So the credit is what a broker would actually fill.

If a quote is missing, one-sided, or too wide to trust, the trade doesn't show up at all. Boring, but honest.

Ask what buyers pay $6.40
Bid what you'd get $6.20

You're selling this leg, so we count $6.20. Not $6.40, not somewhere in between.

Three moves. That's the whole trade.

Every setup we find is the same shape. Once you've seen it once, you'll read every result in seconds.

1

Buy a call near the stock price.

This is your upside. If the stock climbs, this leg climbs with it.

2

Sell a higher call.

It puts a lid on how far the upside goes, and someone pays you for that lid.

3

Sell a put below.

Someone pays you for this too. The catch: if the stock falls that far, you own it at that price. Pick a price you'd be happy to own at.

Together they usually pay you a credit to put the trade on.

What this is. And isn't.

We'd rather you know exactly what you're looking at before you click anything.

It is

  • Priced at the real bid and ask, from live quotes, every time.
  • A scan of about 120 large-cap stocks, twice a day.
  • Upfront about probability, the collateral a trade ties up, and the downside in plain dollars.
  • One strategy, done carefully, instead of forty done shallowly.

It isn't

  • Investment advice. You decide what to trade; we just do the math out loud.
  • A promise of returns. A credit up front is not a guarantee of a profit at the end.
  • Risk-free. The put means you may end up owning the stock. Only sell one at a price you'd be glad to own it at.
  • A broker. You'll place the trade wherever you already trade.
