The credit shown is the credit you could actually collect.

Most screeners price a trade at the last trade or the midpoint, which can be hours stale or simply not fillable. We look at the live quote and use the side of the book you would actually trade on.

When you sell, we use the bid. When you buy, we use the ask. So the credit is what a broker would actually fill.

What this is, and what it isn't.

Options are risky enough without a screener overselling itself. Here is the plain version.

What it is

  • A screener that prices every leg at the real bid and ask, so the credit is one a broker could fill.
  • A scan of about 120 large-cap stocks, twice a day, ranked by credit for the risk.
  • Probability, collateral, and the downside shown in plain dollars on every setup.
  • A tradebook that grades what you saved, so you learn from your own record.

What it isn't

  • Investment advice. It shows you setups; the decision is yours.
  • A promise of returns. Past picks say nothing certain about the next ones.
  • Risk-free. The short put means you may end up owning the stock at the put's strike.
  • A broker. You place the trade with yours, using the numbers here as your checklist.
