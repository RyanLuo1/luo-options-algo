When you sell, we use the bid. When you buy, we use the ask. So the credit is what a broker would actually fill.

Most screeners quote the midpoint or the last trade, which can be hours old. Priced that way, a trade can look like it pays you when it doesn't. We would rather show a smaller number that is true.

One trade, three moves.

The screener looks for a single structure on each stock. It has three legs, and you can read it in a minute.

  1. 1

    Buy a call near the stock price.

    This is the leg you pay for. It gains value if the stock rises.

  2. 2

    Sell a higher call.

    This caps how much you can make, and pays you a premium for agreeing to the cap.

  3. 3

    Sell a put below the stock.

    This pays you a second premium. If the stock falls to that price, you own the shares there.

Together they usually pay you a credit to put the trade on.

What this is, and what it isn't.

It is

  • Priced at the real bid and ask. Sell legs at the bid, the bought leg at the ask.
  • A twice-daily scan of about 120 large-cap stocks for one defined trade.
  • Plain about the odds. Probability of max profit, the collateral tied up, and the downside in dollars.

It isn't

  • Advice. It shows you what the market is offering. Deciding is yours.
  • A promise of returns. We publish the book's record; we don't forecast it.
  • Risk-free. The sold put means you may end up owning the stock. That is the trade.
